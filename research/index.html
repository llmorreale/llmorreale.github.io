Research

Temperate forests of the Eastern US play a critical role in the global carbon cycle and provide essential ecosystem services—yet they face mounting pressures from global change. My research asks how disturbances such as habitat fragmentation, land-use change, invasive pests and pathogens, and a changing climate are altering the structure, function, and carbon dynamics of these forests. By working at multiple scales—from individual forest plots to entire landscapes and regions—I seek to understand not only the local consequences of these stressors, but their aggregate effects on forest carbon budgets and biodiversity across the broader temperate biome.

Answering these questions requires moving beyond traditional field measurements. I combine high-density ground-based forest inventory data with cutting-edge remote sensing technologies—including airborne laser scanning (ALS), terrestrial laser scanning (TLS), and satellite-derived spectral time series—to characterize forest structure and change at scales and resolutions previously impossible. Machine learning and statistical modeling approaches allow me to extract ecologically meaningful signals from these complex, high-dimensional datasets and to generate insights that are both spatially explicit and interpretable.

Projects

Land Use & Forest Fragmentation

Forests across the northeastern US exist in a highly fragmented landscape, where the majority of forest area falls within the influence zone of an edge. Edges are not simply boundaries—they are dynamic zones where microclimatic gradients, altered disturbance regimes, and resource availability interact to produce distinct ecological conditions. My research has demonstrated that these conditions drive elevated tree growth and biomass along forest edges relative to forest interiors, with substantial consequences for regional carbon accounting. At continental scales, using Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) data and satellite-derived time series, I have characterized the spatial extent and carbon contribution of forest edges across the northeastern US. I also examine how trees in urban environments contribute to local carbon budgets and respond to the surrounding land-use mosaic.

Key Publications
Elevated growth and biomass along temperate forest edges Nature Communications, 2021

Using FIA plot data and satellite-derived biomass estimates, I show that forest edges across the northeastern US exhibit significantly elevated tree growth and aboveground biomass—effects that accumulate to meaningfully alter regional carbon budgets. Featured in NPR, EOS, and ScienceDaily.
Edge cases: fragmentation in temperate forest landscapes Frontiers in Ecology & the Environment, 2024

A synthesis of how fragmentation shapes temperate forest structure and function, reviewing evidence for edge-driven changes in carbon dynamics, microclimate, and biodiversity, and identifying key gaps for future research.

Pests, Pathogens & Climate Change

Global change is reshaping temperate forest communities through multiple interacting pathways. Invasive pests, pathogens, and non-native plants—what I term "nuisance species"—are altering tree demography and shifting competitive dynamics in ways that compromise carbon sequestration potential, as documented in ongoing work at the SCBI ForestGEO plot in Front Royal, Virginia (New Phytologist, in revision). Climate-driven changes in forest structure and dynamics are a parallel focus: as part of a pending NSF Long-Term Research in Environmental Biology (LTREB) grant, I am working to disentangle the mechanisms through which global change may be reversing the carbon sink of a temperate deciduous forest. This work leverages terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) to capture detailed three-dimensional forest structure, enabling fine-scale characterization of how changing disturbance regimes alter canopy architecture and carbon dynamics over time.

Forest Habitat & Migratory Bird Conservation

Understanding how forests support wildlife requires moving beyond simple measures of forest cover to characterize the structural and compositional attributes that matter most to biodiversity. In the Appalachian Mountains, I am using airborne laser scanning (ALS) combined with interpretable machine learning methods to identify the specific aspects of forest habitat that support migratory and breeding bird populations across their full annual cycle. By combining high-resolution three-dimensional canopy structure data with bird occurrence records spanning breeding and migration seasons, this work aims to produce ecologically grounded, spatially explicit predictions of habitat quality to inform landscape-scale conservation and management.
