About

I am a Postdoctoral Research Scientist at the University of Maryland Center for Environmental Science – Appalachian Laboratory, and a Research Associate with the Smithsonian Institution's National Zoo and Conservation Biology Institute and at Harvard Forest. My research sits at the intersection of forest ecology, remote sensing, and data science, asking how global change is reshaping the structure, function, and carbon dynamics of temperate forests.

I work at multiple scales—from individual forest plots to landscapes and continental extents—combining long-term field measurements, airborne and terrestrial laser scanning (ALS/TLS), satellite imagery, and machine learning to characterize how fragmentation, land use change, climate change, and invasive pests and pathogens affect forest ecosystems. This work has contributed to our understanding of forest edge effects on carbon uptake, the vulnerability of forest carbon sinks to nuisance species, and how forest habitat structure mediates biodiversity.

I received my Ph.D. in Earth & Environment from Boston University in 2023, where I studied the effects of forest fragmentation on temperate forest growth and structure under the supervision of Lucy Hutyra. Before that, I spent three years at Harvard Forest as a Research Assistant and Lab Manager, and completed my undergraduate degree in Natural Resources at Cornell University.

Research

Temperate forests of the Eastern US play a critical role in the global carbon cycle and provide essential ecosystem services—yet they face mounting pressures from global change. My research asks how disturbances such as habitat fragmentation, land-use change, invasive pests and pathogens, and a changing climate are altering the structure, function, and carbon dynamics of these forests. By working at multiple scales—from individual forest plots to entire landscapes and regions—I seek to understand not only the local consequences of these stressors, but their aggregate effects on forest carbon budgets and biodiversity across the broader temperate biome.

Answering these questions requires moving beyond traditional field measurements. I combine high-density ground-based forest inventory data with cutting-edge remote sensing technologies—including airborne laser scanning (ALS), terrestrial laser scanning (TLS), and satellite-derived spectral time series—to characterize forest structure and change at scales and resolutions previously impossible. Machine learning and statistical modeling approaches allow me to extract ecologically meaningful signals from these complex, high-dimensional datasets and to generate insights that are both spatially explicit and interpretable.

1. Land Use: Edge Effects & Fragmentation

Forests across the northeastern US exist in a highly fragmented landscape, where the majority of forest area falls within the influence zone of an edge. My research has demonstrated that forest edges exhibit elevated tree growth and biomass relative to forest interiors, with important consequences for regional carbon accounting (Morreale et al. 2021, Nature Communications). Working at continental scales using Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) data and satellite time series, I have characterized the spatial extent of forest edges across the northeastern US and quantified their contribution to forest carbon uptake. I also investigate how trees in urban environments contribute to local carbon budgets and are shaped by surrounding land use.

2. Pests, Pathogens & Climate Change

Global change is reshaping temperate forest communities through multiple interacting pathways. Invasive pests, pathogens, and non-native plants are altering tree demography, reducing carbon sequestration potential, and shifting competitive dynamics—as documented in ongoing work at the SCBI ForestGEO plot (New Phytologist, in revision). Climate-driven changes in forest structure and dynamics are another focus: as part of a pending NSF Long-Term Research in Environmental Biology (LTREB) grant, I am working to disentangle the mechanisms through which global change may be reversing the carbon sink of a temperate deciduous forest. This work leverages terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) to capture detailed three-dimensional forest structure, allowing fine-scale characterization of how changing disturbance regimes alter canopy architecture and productivity over time.

3. Forest Habitat & Migratory Bird Conservation

Understanding how forests support wildlife requires moving beyond simple measures of forest cover to characterize the structural and compositional attributes that matter most to biodiversity. In the Appalachian Mountains, I am using airborne laser scanning (ALS) combined with interpretable machine learning methods to identify the specific aspects of forest habitat that support migratory and breeding bird populations across their full annual cycle. This work aims to provide ecologically grounded, spatially explicit predictions of habitat quality to inform conservation and management at landscape scales.
